## Headline

**The clinical content is accurate and genuinely well-sourced.** The CDC
milestone checkpoints, the developmental-screening schedule (9/18/30 months
plus autism-specific 18/24), the DSM-5 ADHD thresholds, the dyslexia
early-marker framing, and the bilingual-language-development claim all check
out against primary sources — several of them verbatim. This is not
AI-generated filler; the curriculum carries real reference lists and cites
them correctly.

Findings are graded:

- **OK** — verified accurate against an authoritative source.
- **Refine** — accurate but the phrasing is looser than the source, or a
  small hedge/caveat would make it safer. Low urgency.
- **Add a note** — the article omits something a clinician would want a
  reader to know. Worth a sentence.
- **Check** — a claim I could not fully verify; flag for the clinical
  reviewer.

## Parent-facing guides

### `child-not-talking-as-much` (featured) — speech milestones

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| "By 18 months, most children can say a handful of single words." | **OK.** CDC 15-month milestone is "tries to say one or two words besides mama/dada"; 18-month is "three or more words." |
| "By 2… short two-word phrases." | **OK.** CDC 2-year: "Says at least two words together, like 'More milk.'" ASHA 19–24 months: "puts two or more words together." |
| "By 3, most children can be understood by people outside their immediate family most of the time." | **Refine.** ASHA's 2023 revision for 2–3 years says only "speech is becoming clearer but may not be understandable to unfamiliar listeners." The older intelligibility literature (~75% intelligible to strangers by 3, ~100% by 4) supports the article's claim, but current ASHA framing pushes reliable stranger-intelligibility toward age 4. Safer: "by 3, roughly three-quarters of what a child says is clear to people outside the family; fully clear speech to strangers is expected closer to 4." |
| Red flags: "no words at all by 18 months, no two-word phrases by 2.5, [understands but] struggles to respond in any way." | **OK.** All are recognised flags and, if anything, slightly conservative (CDC expects word combinations by 24 months, so 30 months is a generous cutoff). |
| "A regression… is always worth a conversation sooner rather than later." | **OK.** Loss of previously acquired language/social skills is a recognised red flag at any age (CDC, AAP). |
| "An initial screening with a speech-language pathologist doesn't require a referral in most cases." | **Check (localisation).** True in much of the US/UK. In Nigeria, direct access varies; on Hayven a parent books an SLP directly, so it holds in context, but a clinician should confirm the general statement is safe for a Nigerian audience. |

### `signs-of-autism-early-childhood`

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| "By 12 months: limited or no response to their name." | **OK** (slightly lenient — CDC flags no response to name by **9** months). |
| "By 12 months: little to no pointing or waving." | **Refine.** CDC flags "does not point to show something interesting **by 18 months**." Protodeclarative pointing often isn't established until 12–15 months, so "little to no pointing by 12 months" over-calls it. Reframe to 15–18 months, or move pointing into the 18–24-month band. |
| "not sharing enjoyment by looking back and forth… (joint attention)." | **OK.** Absent joint attention is one of the more reliable early predictors (CDC "does not share interests by 15 months"). |
| "By 18–24 months: not combining words, echolalia, lining up/ordering objects over pretend play, intense reactions to changes in routine." | **OK.** All map directly to CDC/DSM-5 ASD signs, including "Repeats words or phrases over and over (echolalia)" and "Lines up toys… and gets upset when order is changed." |
| "repetitive movements (hand-flapping, rocking, spinning)." | **OK.** CDC: "Flaps hands, rocks body, or spins self in circles." |
| "an earlier conversation means earlier support, which is consistently linked to better outcomes." | **Refine.** The evidence base is strongest for *earlier access to intervention improving developmental trajectories*; framing it as "better outcomes" is fine, but avoid any implication of "catching up" or "recovery." Current wording is acceptable; a clinician may want to soften "consistently." |
| "It's never too early to ask." | **OK.** Consistent with AAP: screen at 18 and 24 months, or any time there's a concern. |

### `adhd-or-just-a-lot-of-energy`

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| "whether attention and impulse-control struggles show up consistently across more than one setting." | **OK.** DSM-5: symptoms present in **≥ 2 settings**. |
| "(and has for six months or more)." | **OK.** DSM-5: symptoms persisting **≥ 6 months**. |
| "What looks like inattention in a 4-year-old is often just age-typical." | **OK.** AAP evaluates ADHD from age 4, and preschool diagnosis is made cautiously; DSM-5 requires several symptoms before age 12. |
| "an evaluation (usually starting with input from both parents and teachers)." | **OK.** AAP: gather reports from parents/guardians and school staff, use validated rating scales. |
| Overall "not a label to fear, just information." | **OK** — appropriate, non-alarming framing. |

### `dyslexia-warning-signs`

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| "Dyslexia-specific signs tend to show up earlier than reading itself, in how a child hears and plays with the sounds inside words." | **OK.** Phonological-awareness deficits are the core early precursor (IDA; robust from ~age 3). |
| "Before school age: trouble learning nursery rhymes, difficulty noticing that words rhyme, and a family history." | **OK.** All are standard early markers (Reading Rockets, IDA). |
| "dyslexia runs in families more than most learning differences do." | **OK / Refine.** Dyslexia is highly heritable and family history is a strong risk factor; the comparative claim ("more than most") is defensible but a clinician may prefer "dyslexia has a strong hereditary component." |
| "reversing letters or numbers past the age when that's typical (occasional reversals up to around age 7 are normal)." | **OK.** Correctly avoids the common myth that reversals themselves diagnose dyslexia; the "normal up to ~7" hedge matches Reading Rockets. |
| "a child who speaks with an age-appropriate (or advanced) vocabulary but whose reading is well behind that." | **OK.** This "unexpected difficulty" / oral-vs-written gap is central to the IDA definition. |
| "the specific, structured teaching approaches that actually work." | **OK.** Refers to structured literacy / systematic phonics; consider naming it ("structured literacy") so parents can ask for it by name. **Add a note.** |
| Missing: rapid automatised naming (RAN) as an early marker. | **Add a note** (optional). RAN — slow naming of familiar colours/objects/letters — is one of the strongest early predictors and is worth one clause. |

### `sensory-overload-what-it-looks-like`

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| "Sensory overload happens when a child's brain is taking in more… than it can process at once." | **OK** as a lay description. |
| Meltdown ("loud version") vs shutdown ("quiet version"). | **OK / Add a note.** This framing is widely used in autism and OT practice and is consistent with how the field describes it. "Meltdown" and "shutdown" are **practitioner terms, not DSM diagnoses** — fine for a parent guide, but the article shouldn't imply they're formal categories. |
| Tantrum vs meltdown: "a tantrum usually has a goal… and responds to negotiation; a meltdown doesn't have a goal at all." | **OK.** This goal-directed-vs-neurological-overwhelm distinction is the mainstream autism/ABA framing and is well established in practice literature (though the primary-source evidence base is thinner than for the milestone claims). |
| "an occupational therapist who works in sensory processing can help identify specific triggers and build a plan around them." | **Refine + Add a note.** (1) The AAP's 2012 policy statement says **"sensory processing disorder" should not be used as a standalone diagnosis** — sensory-reactivity difficulties are seen across autism, ADHD, developmental coordination disorder, and anxiety. (2) Frequent, intense sensory-overload episodes therefore warrant a **broader developmental screen**, not only an OT sensory assessment. The article should add: "because sensory sensitivities show up in several conditions, a frequent pattern is also worth raising with your paediatrician for a fuller developmental check." (3) The article's framing of the OT role as *identifying triggers and adapting routines/environment* is the well-supported one; it correctly does **not** promise that "sensory integration therapy" will resolve it (AAP: that specific therapy has limited evidence — keep it that way). |

## Cross-cutting observations

1. **Every article already carries the right disclaimer** ("general information, not a diagnosis or medical advice") and consistently routes readers to a professional rather than encouraging home diagnosis. This is the single most important safety property and it's present throughout.

2. **US-centric framing in a Nigerian product.** The clinical *facts* (milestones, screening ages) are internationally applicable, but several articles assume US/UK service structures ("doesn't require a referral," IEP/504 plans in Module 1.5, "insurance reviewer" in Module 5). The Nigeria-specific article (`finding-verified-therapists-lagos`) handles the local context well; the others could use a light localisation pass. Not a clinical-accuracy problem, but worth noting for the clinical reviewer.

3. **Secondary sources.** Module 1 leans on some blog/commercial sources (Skillpoint Therapy, Maryville University, a therapy-provider blog) alongside CDC/StatPearls. Where those support red-flag *checklists a therapist will act on* (1.4), the reviewer should upgrade the citation to a primary source or confirm the thresholds.

4. **"Reviewed by a licensed speech-language pathologist"** appears as the `reviewedBy` line on some parent articles. If that review has not actually happened, the line should be removed until it has — an unbacked "clinically reviewed" claim is a bigger risk than no claim.
